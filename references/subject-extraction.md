# Subject-Specific Extraction

Read only the sections matching the current input. Identify one dominant topology before combining categories.

All numeric ranges in this file are `GUIDANCE` for planning simplification. The user's or source image's exact count, action, relationship, and required information are `MUST` constraints and always override these ranges.

## Universal rule

Separate simplification from omission:

- Simplify anatomy, detail, shading, perspective, and material description.
- Preserve subject type/count, defining diagnostics, action, relationship, setting function, atmosphere, and required information.

Do not omit an environmental object merely because it is not the central subject. Keep it when removal changes the event, use, place, or relationship.

## People

### Single person

Preserve:

- source crop and body read: full body, seated, bust, hands, or partial figure;
- stance, gesture, direction, action, and identity-critical prop;
- source-specific age/body silhouette, hair/headwear mass, clothing blocks, skin/hair contrast, and necessary pattern;
- setting carrier when the person's relation to a place or object defines the image;
- energy through posture, scale, crop, and surrounding rhythm.

Simplify:

- face to a featureless head/skin silhouette unless the current user explicitly requires facial features;
- hair to one or two masses;
- clothing to one to three major fields plus one diagnostic pattern when required;
- limbs, hands, feet, and shoes to blunt masses unless action depends on them;
- line to sparse internal marks for motion, pattern, label, or a necessary structural cue.

Remove eyes, noses, mouths, lashes, blush, hair strands, anatomy outlines, seams, folds, pockets, laces, fingers, jewelry inventory, and fashion-rendering detail when identity survives without them.

Do not change an active pose into a calm pose, change crop arbitrarily, or delete an accepted scene to solve a figure-detail problem.

### Multiple people

Preserve:

- exact count when small, or count band when crowd-scale;
- shared action/container and social topology;
- grouping geometry: circle, row, cluster, opposing sides, repeated windows, or crowd field;
- necessary variation in silhouette, hair/hat, clothing, scale, and position;
- social temperature and density.

Use one body grammar with controlled variation. Remove facial features unless the current user explicitly requires them, and make group figures more pictographic than a single figure. Never collapse a group into one symbolic person or scatter unrelated people as decoration.

## Animals

Build a species checksum from two to five features:

- body and head proportion;
- beak/snout, ear/horn/antler, tail, shell, mane, wing, or leg form;
- diagnostic pattern or color;
- posture, gait, flight phase, or behavior;
- group spacing or habitat relation.

Use broad torso and limb masses, exaggerate one diagnostic feature, and reduce fur/feather/scale rendering to a few pattern fields. Keep habitat when the image is ecological or place-specific. Never turn different species into one rounded mascot body or add human accessories for cuteness.

## Food and cooking

Preserve:

- vessel or support: plate, bowl, pot, tray, package, cup, table, or hand;
- preparation/serving state;
- two to five ingredient diagnostics when needed;
- containment, contact, tool, hand, cutting, pouring, or serving relationships;
- cultural or meal context when indispensable.

Use ingredient colors as marks within or around one dominant vessel/support. Mixed viewpoints are acceptable when relationships remain clear. Do not replace a specific dish with generic fruit, coffee, or cute kitchen props.

## Still life and ordinary objects

Preserve:

- functional relation and support: beside, on, inside, leaning, hanging, stacked, or surrounding;
- one anchor object and enough companions to retain the ritual/use;
- viewpoint when integral;
- one material or pattern cue only when diagnostic.

Convert surfaces to color/pattern blocks and allow awkward scale or mixed viewpoint. Do not turn a relational still life into a random inventory or add generic flowers, stars, hearts, stamps, or stickers.

## Landscapes, nature, and habitats

Preserve an environmental skeleton:

- ground/horizon/sky relation;
- one dominant landmark, enclosure, or natural force;
- one to four supporting built/natural forms;
- weather, time, season, movement, or scale cue;
- people/animal relation when present.

Compress depth into stacked fields, floating landmarks, overlap, or one shallow ground. Use repetition only when it expresses actual plants, waves, snow, stars, flocking, or abundance. Do not replace a specific environment with generic leaves, sun, and one resident character.

## Interiors and functional spaces

Preserve:

- space function;
- enclosure skeleton such as wall/floor fields, window, counter, shelf, side rail, screen, stage, vehicle body, or shared container;
- one to four functional objects that identify the activity;
- subject-to-space relationship: inside, seated on, working at, surrounded by, facing, or moving through.

Flatten walls/floors into large fields and reduce furniture/machinery to frontal slabs or profiles. Do not reconstruct a realistic room, but do not delete architecture when it defines the image.

## Products, packaging, and technology

Preserve:

- silhouette and era-specific geometry;
- orientation, handle/opening/screen/control placement;
- relation to hand, support, packaging, or use context;
- exact label or color block only when it carries identity and is authorized.

Replace unauthorized logos, trademarks, interfaces, serial copy, and mascots with generic geometry or verified text. Do not modernize every device into a rounded app icon or add glossy 3D rendering.

## Abstract or conceptual input

Extract forces instead of mascots:

- dominant direction and flow;
- repetition count or rhythm band;
- collision, enclosure, separation, orbit, stacking, or expansion;
- shape families and scale contrast;
- energy and density.

Reduce shape-family count while preserving force. Do not invent literal characters, objects, badges, or icons to explain an abstract idea.

## Typography-led work

Preserve:

- exact title and required facts;
- language/script, punctuation, reading order, and hierarchy;
- whether text acts as field, frame, label, band, or object;
- relationship between typography and imagery.

Plan type and illustration together. Render required exact copy in the same complete image; if it fails, discard the candidate and generate a new complete image from the original inputs. Do not strip information from an event or information source, accept pseudo-letters, or add a generic headline to a no-text image.

## Mixed-category routing

When several categories appear, choose the dominant relationship:

- person using product → person/action or product/use, depending on the brief;
- animal in habitat → animal checksum plus environmental skeleton;
- food in interior → serving relationship plus minimum setting carriers;
- group in vehicle/room → group topology plus shared enclosure;
- poster containing scene → typography-led only when information hierarchy is indispensable.

Write one combined identity kernel. Do not independently add every category's supports; keep only supports required by the combined relationship.
